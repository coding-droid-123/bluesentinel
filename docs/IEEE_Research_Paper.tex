\begin{abstract}
Marine debris accumulation in underwater environments presents a catastrophic ecological threat that is notoriously difficult to survey and quantify using manual diver inspections or unassisted robotic teleoperation. This paper presents \textbf{Blue Sentinel}, an end-to-end autonomous marine defense and ecological decision-support system that detects, localizes, tracks, and audits submerged anthropogenic debris and marine biodiversity in real time. At the algorithmic core, we fine-tune an \textbf{RT-DETRv4} detector featuring a high-performance \textbf{HGNetV2-L (B4)} convolutional backbone, a decoupled \textbf{HybridEncoder} (AIFI intra-scale self-attention and CCFM cross-scale fusion), and a \textbf{D-FINE} fine-grained distribution refinement decoder across \textbf{22 classes} of the \textbf{TrashCan-Instance} benchmark. Initialized from COCO-pretrained weights and trained for \textbf{58 epochs} on a single NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU under strict batch constraints, the model achieves a mean Average Precision of $\text{AP}@[0.50:0.95] = 0.517$, $\text{AP}@0.50 = 0.686$, and $\text{AP}@0.75 = 0.555$ on the validation split. 

We benchmark Blue Sentinel against the state-of-the-art base paper (\textit{An improved YOLOv11 network for marine debris detection in underwater environment}, Jing et al., \textit{Scientific Reports}, 2026), systematically analyzing the trade-offs between dense single-stage convolutional attention (YOLOv11 + MixStructureBlock + EMA at 416$\times$416, achieving 81.54\% mAP@0.5 at 5.20 MB) and end-to-end transformer set prediction (RT-DETRv4 + D-FINE at 640$\times$640, achieving superior boundary retention and NMS-free high-IoU localization). To bridge laboratory modeling and offshore deployment, we implement an export pipeline compiling the PyTorch model into ONNX and TensorRT FP16 engines with dynamic optimization profiles, achieving real-time inference on edge GPUs. Furthermore, Blue Sentinel introduces an operational decision-support layer: an edge-in-the-loop \textbf{ROV Simulator} with real-time video streaming, a multi-tier persistence-gated alert engine that detects critical wildlife entanglements and ghost fishing gear, a density-weighted debris pile ranking algorithm, and a parametric \textbf{Cleanup Cost and Impact Optimizer} providing range-based operational budgeting. The complete platform is deployed via a FastAPI backend, PostgreSQL 18 with pgvector spatial persistence, a React 19 dashboard, and local Llama-3.1-8B LLM ecological audit synthesis over an encrypted zero-trust hybrid mesh.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Marine debris contamination in marine benthic and pelagic zones has emerged as one of the most pressing anthropogenic threats to global ocean ecosystems. Millions of metric tons of non-biodegradable plastics, synthetic polymers, discarded fishing gear, and industrial waste enter marine environments annually \cite{trashcan}. Beyond visual degradation, submerged debris inflicts severe ecological mortality: ghost fishing gear (nets, ropes, longlines) continuously ensnares fish, marine mammals, and benthic organisms; degrading plastics leach persistent toxic additives into coastal food webs; and submerged debris piles smother delicate coral reefs and seagrass meadows \cite{trashcan}, \cite{jaffe}.

Quantifying and mitigating this crisis requires frequent, wide-area underwater visual monitoring. However, conventional manual visual surveys conducted by scientific divers or teleoperated Remotely Operated Vehicles (ROVs) are inherently slow, hazardous, expensive, and spatially localized. Automated computer-vision systems deployed on ROVs and Autonomous Underwater Vehicles (AUVs) offer a scalable alternative, but underwater optical imagery presents severe domain-specific degradation mechanisms \cite{jaffe}, \cite{yolo11_base}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Wavelength-Dependent Optical Attenuation:} Seawater exponentially absorbs longer wavelengths (reds and yellows within the first 3--5 meters of water depth), heavily skewing ambient illumination into a low-contrast, monochromatic cyan/blue spectrum \cite{jaffe}.
    \item \textbf{Backscatter and Turbidity:} Suspended organic particulates (``marine snow'') and floating silt scatter artificial ROV illuminators, creating severe backscatter haze, non-uniform vignetting, and obscured object silhouettes.
    \item \textbf{Deformability and Benthic Crypsis:} Unlike rigid terrestrial objects, underwater debris items such as submerged tarps, fragmented packaging, and frayed ropes undergo continuous morphological deformation with currents and partial sediment burial. Simultaneously, benthic fauna (crabs, flatfish, starfish) have evolved intricate camouflage textures that mimic the seafloor substrate \cite{trashcan}, \cite{yolo11_base}.
\end{enumerate}

Recent deep learning approaches on benchmark datasets such as \textbf{TrashCan} \cite{trashcan} have explored single-stage convolutional detectors (YOLO variants) \cite{yolov3}, \cite{yolo11_base} and region-based detectors (Faster R-CNN) \cite{fasterrcnn}. Most notably, the foundational literature by Jing et al. (\textit{Scientific Reports}, 2026) \cite{yolo11_base} engineered an improved YOLOv11 network integrating \textbf{MixStructureBlock} and \textbf{Efficient Multi-Scale Attention (EMA)} \cite{ema}, achieving remarkable detection recall and lightweight efficiency on 416$\times$416 inputs. However, anchor-based and dense CNN architectures rely on hand-crafted Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) heuristics, which struggle with sprawling, overlapping debris piles, and regress deterministic bounding box coordinates that frequently degrade under strict spatial overlap thresholds ($\text{IoU} \ge 0.75$).

To address these fundamental limitations and advance practical ocean conservation, this paper presents \textbf{Blue Sentinel}, an autonomous end-to-end marine debris detection, edge optimization, and ecological decision-support platform. Blue Sentinel adopts the state-of-the-art \textbf{RT-DETRv4} transformer architecture \cite{rtdetrv4} combined with \textbf{D-FINE} fine-grained distribution refinement \cite{dfine} and an \textbf{HGNetV2-L} backbone. By supervising bounding-box edge predictions as continuous probability distributions over discrete distance bins, the model eliminates NMS post-processing and delivers exceptional boundary localization precision.

\textbf{Key Contributions of This Work:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Empirical Benchmarking on TrashCan-Instance (22 Classes):} We adapt and fine-tune RT-DETRv4 with D-FINE on the 22-class TrashCan-Instance dataset for 58 epochs, achieving $\text{AP}@[0.50:0.95] = 0.517$, $\text{AP}@0.50 = 0.686$, and $\text{AP}@0.75 = 0.555$, demonstrating near-perfect precision on rigid debris (up to 1.000 AP@0.50) while rigorously diagnosing boundary failure modes.
    \item \textbf{In-Depth Comparative Analysis Against Foundational Literature:} We present a rigorous comparative study against the state-of-the-art base paper by Jing et al. (\textit{Scientific Reports}, 2026) \cite{yolo11_base}, contrasting dense convolutional multi-scale attention (YOLOv11 + MixStructureBlock + EMA) with transformer set prediction (RT-DETRv4 + D-FINE).
    \item \textbf{Edge Optimization \& High-Throughput Deployment Pipeline:} We implement an end-to-end model conversion pipeline compiling the PyTorch model into ONNX and TensorRT FP16 execution engines with dynamic input profiling, alongside multi-threaded CPU fallbacks and zero-network local caching.
    \item \textbf{Operational Decision Support \& Edge-in-the-Loop ROV Simulation:} Moving beyond passive object classification, we engineer an operational decision layer featuring an edge-in-the-loop ROV simulator, a persistence-gated multi-tier alert engine (surfacing wildlife entanglement, ghost gear, and large debris piles), a weighted debris pile ranking algorithm, and a parametric Cleanup Cost and Impact Optimizer providing range-based operational budgeting.
    \item \textbf{Full-Stack Deployment \& Diagnostics:} We document a hybrid edge-cloud deployment topology (FastAPI, React 19, PostgreSQL 18 with pgvector, and local Llama-3.1-8B LLM ecological audit synthesis via Tailscale/Cloudflare), accompanied by a comprehensive diagnostic catalog resolving non-obvious runtime and training failures in transformer-based detectors.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work \& Comparative Literature}

\subsection{Underwater Visual Object Detection}
Underwater computer vision has evolved from classical hand-crafted color-correction heuristics toward deep convolutional and transformer-based networks. The TrashCan dataset by Hong et al. \cite{trashcan} established standard evaluation protocols across 22 instance classes. Early baseline evaluations utilized Faster R-CNN \cite{fasterrcnn} and SSD \cite{ssd}. While accurate, two-stage detectors suffer from high inference latency ($>$100 ms), rendering them impractical for resource-constrained ROVs.

Subsequent research focused heavily on single-stage YOLO architectures \cite{yolov3}. Most recently, Jing et al. \cite{yolo11_base} (\textit{Scientific Reports}, 2026, 16:7074) developed an enhanced YOLOv11 detector specifically optimized for underwater environments. Their work introduced the \textbf{MixStructureBlock}, replacing standard residual blocks with multi-branch dilated convolutions and composite channel-pixel attention to combat underwater haze, and integrated \textbf{Efficient Multi-Scale Attention (EMA)} \cite{ema} to enhance weak feature extraction. While achieving an impressive 81.54\% mAP@0.5 with a compact 5.20 MB model, dense single-stage CNNs remain constrained by deterministic coordinate regression and IoU-based NMS, which can cause duplicate detections or missed objects in dense debris piles.

\subsection{Real-Time Detection Transformers (DETR)}
The Detection Transformer (DETR) paradigm pioneered by Carion et al. \cite{detr} reformulated object detection as direct set prediction, eliminating hand-crafted priors such as anchor boxes and NMS via bipartite matching with the Hungarian algorithm. RT-DETR \cite{rtdetr} achieved the first real-time transformer detector by coupling a convolutional backbone with an efficient hybrid encoder that decouples intra-scale self-attention from cross-scale feature fusion.

D-FINE \cite{dfine} overhauled the bounding box regression head in DETR architectures. Traditional detectors regress four scalar offsets $(c_x, c_y, w, h)$. D-FINE reformulates regression as Fine-Grained Localization (FGL), modeling each bounding box boundary offset as a continuous probability distribution across discrete distance bins. RT-DETRv4 \cite{rtdetrv4} extends this framework with an HGNetV2 backbone, support for vision foundation model distillation, and structural re-parameterization.

\subsection{Attention Mechanisms: EMA vs. AIFI Deformable Attention}
Attention mechanisms in underwater vision serve to filter severe backscatter noise and accentuate weak object boundaries:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Efficient Multi-Scale Attention (EMA) \cite{ema}:} As deployed in the base paper \cite{yolo11_base}, EMA groups feature channels into sub-spaces, extracts horizontal and vertical 1D directional global pooling representations, and computes cross-dimensional attention maps modulated by Group Normalization and Sigmoid gating.
    \item \textbf{AIFI Intra-Scale Attention \cite{rtdetr}, \cite{rtdetrv4}:} In RT-DETRv4, Attention-based Intra-scale Feature Interaction (AIFI) applies multi-head self-attention exclusively to the deepest feature map ($P_5$). By operating on compact spatial tokens (20$\times$20), AIFI captures global receptive fields across the entire visual scene with minimal computational overhead.
    \item \textbf{Deformable Cross-Attention \cite{dfine}, \cite{rtdetrv4}:} In the DFINETransformer decoder, 300 learnable object queries interact with multi-scale feature pyramids through deformable cross-attention, sampling only a sparse set of key locations per query rather than attending to all tokens, thereby drastically reducing computational complexity while retaining fine spatial detail.
\end{itemize}

\section{Dataset \& Annotation Preprocessing}

\subsection{The TrashCan-Instance Benchmark}
We utilize the \textbf{TrashCan-Instance} dataset \cite{trashcan} in MS-COCO format \cite{coco}, comprising 7,212 underwater training images and 1,146 validation images across \textbf{22 classes} categorized into three semantic domains:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Equipment (1 class):} \texttt{rov} (survey hardware, robotic arms, thrusters).
    \item \textbf{Marine Organisms \& Flora (7 classes):} \texttt{plant}, \texttt{animal\_fish}, \texttt{animal\_starfish}, \texttt{animal\_shells}, \texttt{animal\_crab}, \texttt{animal\_eel}, \texttt{animal\_etc}.
    \item \textbf{Anthropogenic Debris (14 classes):} \texttt{trash\_clothing}, \texttt{trash\_pipe}, \texttt{trash\_bottle}, \texttt{trash\_bag}, \texttt{trash\_snack\_wrapper}, \texttt{trash\_can}, \texttt{trash\_cup}, \texttt{trash\_container}, \texttt{trash\_unknown\_instance}, \texttt{trash\_branch}, \texttt{trash\_wreckage}, \texttt{trash\_tarp}, \texttt{trash\_rope}, \texttt{trash\_net}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Preprocessing \& Zero-Indexing Normalization}
The source TrashCan-Instance annotations utilize 1-indexed category identifiers ($\text{category\_id} \in [1, 22]$). In the RT-DETRv4 training configuration, MS-COCO category remapping is disabled (\texttt{remap\_mscoco\_category: False}) to preserve custom category semantics. Consequently, the data loader passes raw JSON \texttt{category\_id} values directly to the classification loss head. Passing 1-indexed IDs to a 22-way classifier produces label indices spanning $[1, 22]$, exceeding the valid range of $[0, 21]$. This mismatch triggers a fatal CUDA device-side assertion failure inside the Hungarian matcher cost calculation.

To prevent this defect, we developed an automated preprocessing pipeline that standardizes all \texttt{categories[].id} and annotation \texttt{category\_id} attributes to a contiguous 0-indexed range $[0, 21]$. A unified, deterministic mapping dictionary was shared across both training and validation splits to guarantee absolute cross-split semantic alignment.

\textbf{1) Backbone --- HGNetV2-B4:}
The High-Performance GPU Network V2 (HGNetV2, B4 variant) extracts multi-scale features at strides $\{8, 16, 32\}$. Key internal modules include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{StemBlock:} Multi-branch entry module with four sequential convolutions and MaxPool2d providing rapid $4\times$ spatial downsampling while preserving high-frequency edge information.
    \item \textbf{HG\_Block (Hourglass Block):} Core building block utilizing multi-branch convolutions with channel-split residual connections to extract deep hierarchical representations.
    \item \textbf{EseModule (Effective Squeeze-and-Excitation):} Lightweight channel attention replacing standard two-layer fully-connected bottlenecks with a single $1\times1$ convolution and Sigmoid gating.
    \item \textbf{LearnableAffineBlock (LAB):} Per-channel learnable affine transformation layers ($\text{scale} \cdot x + \text{bias}$) that accelerate gradient convergence across deep stages.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{2) Neck --- Decoupled HybridEncoder:}
The HybridEncoder decouples intra-scale self-attention from cross-scale feature fusion:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{AIFI:} Global self-attention is applied exclusively to the deepest feature map $P_5$ ($20\times20 = 400$ tokens at $640\times640$ input) with 8 attention heads ($\text{hidden\_dim}=256$, $\text{FFN}=1024$), capturing global context without quadratic memory explosion ($O(N^2)$).
    \item \textbf{CCFM:} Features are fused bidirectionally via top-down FPN \cite{fpn} and bottom-up PAN \cite{pan} using \texttt{RepNCSPELAN4} blocks (CSP-ELAN with VGGBlock bottlenecks and structural re-parameterization) and \texttt{SCDown} spatial-channel downsampling modules.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{3) Decoder --- DFINETransformer with Distribution Refinement:}
A 6-layer deformable attention transformer decoder processes 300 learnable object queries. D-FINE reformulates bounding box regression by modeling each boundary offset $e_j \in \{l, t, r, b\}$ as a continuous probability distribution over $N_{\text{reg}} = 32$ discrete distance bins:
\begin{equation}
e_j = \sum_{k=0}^{N_{\text{reg}}} k \cdot \text{softmax}\left(\frac{w_{j,k}}{\tau}\right) \cdot s
\label{eq:dfine}
\end{equation}
where $w_{j,k}$ are query logits for boundary $j$ at bin $k$, $\tau = 1.0$ is the distribution temperature, and $s = 4.0$ is the regression scale. Contrastive denoising training is incorporated with 100 denoising queries, a label noise ratio of 0.5, and a box noise scale of 1.0.

\textbf{4) Multi-Task Loss Formulation:}
The network is optimized using a composite multi-task objective:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \lambda_1 \mathcal{L}_{\text{VFL}} + \lambda_2 \mathcal{L}_{\text{L1}} + \lambda_3 \mathcal{L}_{\text{GIoU}} + \lambda_4 \mathcal{L}_{\text{FGL}} + \lambda_5 \mathcal{L}_{\text{DDF}}
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{VFL}}$ is Varifocal Loss \cite{varifocal}, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{L1}}$ is smooth L1 distance, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{GIoU}}$ is Generalized IoU loss \cite{giou}, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{FGL}}$ is Fine-Grained Localization loss supervising edge distance distributions, and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{DDF}}$ is Dense Distribution Fusion loss distilling edge representations across decoder layers. We set loss weights:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_1 = 1.0, \quad \lambda_2 = 5.0, \quad \lambda_3 = 2.0, \quad \lambda_4 = 0.15, \quad \lambda_5 = 1.5
\label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}
Hungarian matching cost weights are: $\text{class} = 2.0$, $\text{bbox} = 5.0$, and $\text{GIoU} = 2.0$.

\textbf{Augmentation Schedule Rationale:} Underwater visual representations benefit from heavy composite augmentation during initial feature extraction, but fine-grained bounding-box probability distributions degrade if trained under extreme affine distortion near convergence. We implement a staged schedule: random photometric distortion (jittering brightness $[0.875, 1.125]$, contrast $[0.5, 1.5]$, and hue $[-0.05, 0.05]$ to emulate underwater spectral shifts) and Mosaic are applied from epoch 0. Mixup is activated at epoch 5 and terminated at epoch 29. Crucially, all augmentations are disabled at epoch 48 (\texttt{stop\_epoch: 48}), providing an augmentation-free fine-tuning tail over the final $\sim$17\% of training to stabilize edge distribution representations.

\section{Edge Optimization \& Model Export Pipeline}
Deploying vision transformers onto marine robotic platforms requires eliminating Python runtime overhead. We developed a multi-format conversion and acceleration pipeline:

\subsection{End-to-End ONNX Graph Export}
We engineered an \texttt{ExportWrapper} module in \texttt{backend/convert\_pth\_to\_onnx.py} that fuses the deployed RT-DETRv4 model with its native \texttt{PostProcessor} into a single unified computational graph:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Traced with ONNX Opset 16, embedding dynamic batch dimensions across two runtime inputs: \texttt{images} ($[B, 3, 640, 640]$) and \texttt{orig\_target\_sizes} ($[B, 2]$).
    \item The graph computes the integral expectation (Eq.~\ref{eq:dfine}) directly inside the ONNX graph and denormalizes bounding box coordinates to native input image dimensions.
    \item Outputs three clean, tensor-native arrays: \texttt{labels} ($[B, 300]$), \texttt{boxes} ($[B, 300, 4]$ in $[x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2]$ coordinates), and \texttt{scores} ($[B, 300]$), completely eliminating client-side decoding dependencies.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{TensorRT FP16 Engine Generation}
In \texttt{backend/convert\_onnx\_to\_engine.py}, the ONNX graph is compiled into a serialized TensorRT engine:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Precision:} Compiled with native FP16 Tensor Core execution flags (\texttt{trt.BuilderFlag.FP16}), halving VRAM footprint to $\sim$1.8 GB and doubling inference throughput.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Profiles:} Configures explicit optimization profiles: $\text{images}: \min=(1, 3, 640, 640), \text{opt}=(1, 3, 640, 640), \max=(B_{\max}, 3, 640, 640)$ and $\text{orig\_target\_sizes}: \min=(1, 2), \text{opt}=(1, 2), \max=(B_{\max}, 2)$.
    \item \textbf{Memory Pool:} Allocates 4 GB of workspace memory to fuse multi-head attention kernels, layer norms, and convolutional layers into unified CUDA execution blocks.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Multi-Threaded CPU Fallback \& Local Caching}
Under CPU execution mode, the engine dynamically queries physical core counts via \texttt{multiprocessing.cpu\_count()} and configures PyTorch intra-op and inter-op execution threads (\texttt{torch.set\_num\_threads(n\_cores)}). Furthermore, the model loader implements a zero-network local cache resolver (\texttt{\_resolve\_checkpoint}): if fine-tuned weights exist locally in \texttt{backend/weights/best.pth}, the model loads immediately with zero remote HTTP calls, ensuring rapid offline boots on sea vessels without internet connectivity.

\section{Operational Scaling \& Decision Support Layer}

\subsection{Edge-in-the-Loop ROV Simulator \& Multi-Object Tracking (MOT)}
To evaluate the platform under operational conditions without requiring access to an active offshore research vessel, we developed an edge-in-the-loop \textbf{ROV Simulator} (\texttt{frontend/src/components/ROVSimulator.jsx}):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Video File Ingestion \& Browser-Side Frame Extraction:} The simulator accepts locally-uploaded ROV footage files via the browser File API. Supported container formats include \textbf{MP4 (H.264/AVC)}, \textbf{WebM (VP8/VP9)}, and \textbf{MOV (H.264)}; MPEG-4 Part 2 (FMP4) streams are rejected with an explicit codec error message since Chromium's media decoder does not support them. There is \textbf{no hard file size ceiling}: the \texttt{createObjectURL} API streams the file directly from local storage into an HTML5 \texttt{<video>} element without buffering to RAM, making it practical to analyze multi-gigabyte deep-dive recordings. Frame extraction operates entirely in browser memory via HTML5 Canvas rasterization (\texttt{canvas.drawImage}), compressing the raw video frame into a JPEG blob at quality 0.85 without any intermediate filesystem operations.
    \item \textbf{Synchronized Step-Through and Continuous Inference Modes:} The simulator exposes two operational modes: (i) \emph{Synchronized Step-Through} pauses the video at each extracted position, dispatches inference, holds bounding box overlays for an inspector-configurable dwell time (400 ms--2 s), then advances by a configurable temporal step (0.2 s--2 s); (ii) \emph{Continuous Slow-Motion} plays the video at a reduced rate (0.10$\times$--1.0$\times$) and dispatches frames at a configurable cadence (continuous to every 2 s). Both modes share the same frame extraction and MOT pipeline.
    \item \textbf{Zero-Disk Streaming Endpoint:} Utilizes a dedicated \texttt{POST /detect?stream=true} endpoint in the FastAPI backend that bypasses synchronous database persistence during active playback, sustaining low-latency telemetry streaming.
    \item \textbf{Mission Lifecycle Registration:} At simulation start, the ROV Simulator issues \texttt{POST /missions/start}, registering a \texttt{SurveyMission} record and receiving a persistent \texttt{mission\_id} UUID. This identifier is forwarded to all subsequent \texttt{POST /incidents} calls, enabling end-to-end relational traceability from detected events back to their parent survey transect in the database.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Object Tracking (MOT) via Spatial IoU Association:} Naive per-frame summation drastically inflates debris censuses by counting stationary or slow-moving items across multiple frames. To eliminate duplicate counting, Blue Sentinel incorporates an online spatial tracking algorithm:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{IoU}(b_{\text{det}}, b_{\text{track}}) = \frac{\text{Area}(b_{\text{det}} \cap b_{\text{track}})}{\text{Area}(b_{\text{det}} \cup b_{\text{track}})} \ge 0.35
    \label{eq:iou_track}
    \end{equation}
    For every frame $f$, detections are matched greedily to active object tracks sharing the identical category label $c$. Matched tracks inherit the persistent trajectory identifier $\text{ID}_k$, update coordinates $b_{\text{track}} \leftarrow b_{\text{det}}$, and reset their lost counter ($k_{\text{lost}} = 0$). Unmatched detections instantiate new tracks, incrementing the cumulative unique debris counter $N_{\text{unique}}$. Tracks unobserved for more than $k_{\text{lost}} > 8$ consecutive frames are pruned from active memory.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Persistence-Gated Multi-Tier Alert Engine}
Blue Sentinel implements a multi-tier alert engine gated by \textbf{temporal persistence} across successive video frames (Table~\ref{tab:alert_taxonomy}). The persistence gate prevents spurious single-frame co-occurrences from generating false critical alerts.

Specifically, for wildlife entanglement classification, a co-occurrence of a fauna bounding box (any class in \{\texttt{animal\_*}\}) and ghost gear (\texttt{trash\_net}, \texttt{trash\_rope}, \texttt{trash\_tarp}) is observed but \textbf{only escalated to a Critical ENTANGLEMENT alert after the co-occurrence persists across at least 3 consecutive inference frames}. Let $k_{\text{entangle}}(a, g, f)$ denote the frame counter for the $(\text{animal}~a, \text{gear}~g)$ pair at frame $f$:
\begin{equation}
k_{\text{entangle}}(a, g, f) = \begin{cases} k_{\text{entangle}}(a, g, f-1) + 1 & \text{if } f - f_{\text{last}} \le 2 \\ 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\label{eq:entangle_gate}
\end{equation}
An ENTANGLEMENT incident is fired and logged to \texttt{POST /incidents} only when $k_{\text{entangle}}(a, g, f) \ge 3$. Each incident is posted at most once per 6-frame window (\texttt{lastAlertFrame} guard), preventing database flooding during long sequences. The \texttt{animal\_species} and \texttt{gear\_type} fields are populated directly from the detection class labels for full relational traceability.

\subsection{Density-Weighted Debris Pile Ranking}
Blue Sentinel ranks detected debris clusters using a multi-factor ecological severity score:
\begin{equation}
\text{Severity Score} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w(c_i) \cdot s_i
\label{eq:severity_score}
\end{equation}
where $s_i$ is model confidence, and $w(c_i)$ is an ecological risk weight: $w=3.0$ for ghost nets and heavy ropes; $w=2.5$ for submerged tarps; $w=2.0$ for industrial wreckage and pipes; $w=1.5$ for plastic containers, cans, and bags; $w=1.0$ for consumer bottles and clothing; $w=0.8$ for organic branches and wrappers. Clusters are sorted descending by score, allowing operators to prioritize high-yield recovery sites.

\subsection{Parametric Cleanup Cost \& Impact Optimizer}
In \texttt{frontend/src/components/CleanupOptimizer.jsx}, the platform incorporates a parametric Cleanup Cost and Impact Optimizer:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Equipment Mapping:} Debris classes automatically dictate equipment requirements (nets require hydraulic shears and lift bags; wreckage requires heavy lift rigging; small plastics require collection sacks).
    \item \textbf{Parametric Crew-Hour Modeling:} Each debris class is assigned editable operational recovery time intervals $[t_{\min}, t_{\max}]$ (in hours per item) and minimum required crew personnel $C_c$.
    \item \textbf{Range-Based Uncertainty Propagation:} The optimizer reports costs and crew hours strictly as ranges:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{Hours}_{\text{total}} = \left[ \sum_{c} t_{\min}(c) \cdot N_{\min}(c), \quad \sum_{c} t_{\max}(c) \cdot N_{\max}(c) \right]
    \label{eq:hours}
    \end{equation}
    \begin{equation}
    \text{Cost}_{\text{total}} = \text{Hours}_{\text{total}} \times C_{\max} \times R_{\text{crew}} + \text{Cost}_{\text{vessel}}
    \label{eq:cost}
    \end{equation}
    where $R_{\text{crew}}$ is the hourly labor rate (\$45/hr baseline) and $\text{Cost}_{\text{vessel}}$ represents fixed vessel transit expenses. This range-based formulation explicitly avoids promising unvalidated exact figures.
\end{enumerate}

\section{System Architecture \& Deployment Topology}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:pipeline} details the complete computational sequence in Blue Sentinel.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Blue Sentinel Real-Time Inference, Alerting, and Audit Pipeline}
\label{alg:pipeline}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Raw underwater frame $I \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times H \times W}$, confidence threshold $\tau = 0.40$, stream flag $f_{\text{stream}}$, threat taxonomy $\mathcal{M}_{\text{threat}}$, deployed weights $\mathcal{W}$, active mission $m_{\text{id}}$.
\ENSURE Filtered detections $\mathcal{D}^*$, alert set $\mathcal{A}$, database record $\mathcal{B}_{\text{id}}$, downloadable audit report $\mathcal{R}_{\text{pdf}}$.
\STATE Register survey session via \texttt{POST /missions/start} $\to$ receive $m_{\text{id}}$.
\STATE Ingest frame $I$ and record native dimensions $(W_{\text{orig}}, H_{\text{orig}})$.
\STATE Bilinearly resize $I$ to $640 \times 640$ and linearly scale pixel values to $[0.0, 1.0]$.
\STATE Transfer tensor to CUDA with \texttt{torch.autocast(dtype=float16)}.
\STATE Extract multi-scale feature pyramid $\{P_3, P_4, P_5\}$ via HGNetV2-B4.
\STATE Apply AIFI self-attention to $P_5$ and CCFM cross-scale fusion $\to F_{\text{fused}}$.
\STATE Decode 300 queries via DFINETransformer $\to$ logits $S \in \mathbb{R}^{300 \times 22}$, edges $e_j$.
\STATE Reconstruct boxes via Eq.~(\ref{eq:dfine}) and filter: $\mathcal{D}^* = \{(\operatorname{argmax}(S_k), \max(S_k), b_k) \mid \max(S_k) \ge \tau\}$.
\STATE Denormalize coordinates to native $(W_{\text{orig}}, H_{\text{orig}})$.
\STATE Assign threat tiers: $\theta_k = \mathcal{M}_{\text{threat}}(c_k) \in \{\text{Critical, High, Medium, Low}\}$.
\STATE Evaluate real-time alerts $\mathcal{A}$ via Table~\ref{tab:alert_taxonomy}.
\STATE Compute weighted debris pile severity score via Eq.~(\ref{eq:severity_score}).
\IF{$f_{\text{stream}}$ is True}
    \STATE Return JSON $\{\text{image}: \text{B64}, \text{detections}: \mathcal{D}^*, \text{alerts}: \mathcal{A}\}$ directly.
\ELSE
    \STATE Insert \texttt{ScanBatch} and \texttt{Detection} records into PostgreSQL 18 asynchronously.
    \STATE Synthesize ecological audit via local Llama-3.1 LLM and render PDF report $\mathcal{R}_{\text{pdf}}$.
\ENDIF
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Backend Engine \& Three-Pillar Mission Database Architecture}
The backend is built upon an asynchronous FastAPI engine serving RT-DETRv4 ONNX Runtime sessions alongside an ACID-compliant PostgreSQL 18 persistence layer. Rather than logging every video frame—which would cause database bloating and disk exhaustion during continuous underwater operation—Blue Sentinel adopts a \textbf{Three-Pillar Mission Architecture}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pillar 1: Survey Missions \& Transects (\texttt{survey\_missions}):} Lifecycle-managed via two endpoints. \texttt{POST /missions/start} registers a new \texttt{SurveyMission} record at simulation launch and returns a \texttt{mission\_id} UUID used to correlate all incidents occurring during that session. \texttt{POST /missions/finish} finalizes the record at session end, persisting: total transect duration (seconds), processed frame count, cumulative unique tracked debris count ($N_{\text{unique}}$), average inference FPS, model execution backend (ONNX Runtime / TensorRT), average latency (ms), and class-disaggregated debris frequency histograms \texttt{(summary\_by\_class)}.
    \item \textbf{Pillar 2: Ecological Emergency Incidents \& Hotspots (\texttt{mission\_incidents}, \texttt{debris\_hotspots}):} Persists high-priority environmental emergencies triggered by the persistence-gated alert engine (\texttt{POST /incidents}). When a confirmed ghost gear event or wildlife entanglement is detected (persistence gate $\ge 3$ frames, throttled to 1 post per 6-frame window), the system extracts a compressed JPEG snapshot crop of the incident frame and logs the timestamp, incident classification, severity level, affected marine fauna (\texttt{animal\_species}), and physical gear characteristics (\texttt{gear\_type}). Concurrently, top debris pile clusters exceeding density thresholds are stored in \texttt{debris\_hotspots} with coordinates and class hazard rankings.
    \item \textbf{Pillar 3: Marine Remediation Zones \& Parametric Logistics (\texttt{cleanup\_zones}, \texttt{cleanup\_plans}):} Maintains operational inventories for surveyed maritime regions, tracking debris distributions, transit expenses, and priority indices. The optimizer endpoint (\texttt{POST /cleanup/optimize}) evaluates editable parametric diver recovery rates to calculate bounded crew-hour intervals, required recovery equipment, and total operational cost ranges, persisting finalized remediation plans into \texttt{cleanup\_plans}. The \texttt{GET /cleanup/zones} endpoint exposes both \texttt{location\_coords} and a frontend-compatible \texttt{location} alias for seamless integration with the React dashboard.
\end{enumerate}
Additionally, a Zero-Trust Hybrid Mesh links the public React frontend with edge servers via a Cloudflare Zero-Trust tunnel, while LLM reporting workloads are secured over an encrypted Tailscale WireGuard overlay network.

\subsection{Local Generative AI Ecological Audit Synthesis}
Blue Sentinel integrates local Generative AI powered by \textbf{Ollama} running \textbf{Llama-3.1 (8B)} quantized to Q4\_K\_M ($\sim$4.8 GB memory footprint). The LLM processes structured telemetry JSON summaries to synthesize natural-language ecological risk narratives, highlight marine fauna hazards, and formulate mitigation recommendations without external cloud API dependencies.

\subsection{Ecological Audit Report Generation}
Following a completed ROV survey session, Blue Sentinel synthesizes a comprehensive \textbf{downloadable PDF ecological audit report} via the \texttt{ReportPanel} component (\texttt{frontend/src/components/ReportPanel.jsx}):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Data Aggregation:} The report ingests the finalized mission summary (class tallies, unique object count, inference FPS, mission duration) and any triggered incidents from the current session.
    \item \textbf{LLM Narrative Synthesis:} Structured telemetry is forwarded to the local Llama-3.1-8B instance, which generates a natural-language ecological risk narrative covering: species-specific entanglement hazards, highest-density debris classes, estimated persistence risk, and recommended remediation priorities.
    \item \textbf{PDF Rendering:} The structured HTML report document (including summary tables, severity breakdowns, and LLM narrative) is rasterized into a print-quality PDF using \texttt{html2canvas} (high-DPI screenshot capture) and \texttt{jsPDF} (PDF assembly), providing a compliance-ready audit trail for environmental program managers and cleanup contractors.
    \item \textbf{Zero External Dependency:} All report generation occurs fully client-side (browser) or on the local edge server. No data is transmitted to external cloud APIs, satisfying maritime data sovereignty requirements for offshore research platforms.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Interactive Presentation Dashboard}
The React 19 dashboard provides an interactive inspection canvas with bounding box overlays, category filtering, and real-time confidence thresholding, alongside automated PDF report export via \texttt{html2canvas} and \texttt{jsPDF}.

\section{Analysis and Discussion}

\subsection{Boundary Stability in Rigid Debris}
The top cluster (\texttt{trash\_pipe}, \texttt{rov}, \texttt{trash\_bottle}, \texttt{trash\_wreckage}) benefits from rigid silhouettes and high contrast against seabed silt. Under D-FINE distribution regression (Eq.~\ref{eq:dfine}), edge logits converge to sharp unimodal peaks, resulting in 90.9\%--93.0\% retention from $\text{AP}@0.50$ to $\text{AP}@0.75$.

\subsection{Anthropogenic Debris vs. Marine Life Divide}
The 16.5-point gap in mean AP@[.50:.95] between debris ($0.556$) and marine life ($0.391$) stems from benthic crypsis (e.g., crabs and shells blending into seabed rock) and depth-dependent red light attenuation \cite{jaffe}.

\subsection{Localization Collapse in Deformable Morphologies}
A notable phenomenon is the localization collapse observed in \texttt{animal\_fish} ($-49.1\%$), \texttt{animal\_starfish} ($-43.8\%$), and \texttt{trash\_net} ($-35.9\%$). While their gross presence is easily spotted ($\text{AP}@0.50 = 0.861$ for starfish and $0.899$ for nets), their non-convex, porous geometry introduces rectangular bounding box slack, heavily penalized at $\text{IoU} \ge 0.75$.

\subsection{Detection-Bound vs. Localization-Bound Plateau}
Three classes (\texttt{trash\_snack\_wrapper}, \texttt{trash\_clothing}, \texttt{trash\_cup}) exhibit zero precision loss between $\text{AP}@0.50$ and $\text{AP}@0.75$ ($\Delta\text{AP} = 0.000$, $100\%$ retention). These classes are detection-bound: once discovered against biofouling, their boundaries are localized with high certainty.

\subsection{Extreme Amorphous Debris: trash\_tarp Failure Mode}
\texttt{trash\_tarp} forms the global minimum ($\text{AP@[.50:.95]} = 0.158$) due to extreme topological plasticity, draping over benthic contours, and sediment burial obscuring $>70\%$ of its surface.

\section{Implementation Notes \& Runtime Diagnostics}

\subsection{Category ID Off-by-One Mismatch}
With \texttt{remap\_mscoco\_category: False}, 1-indexed annotations produce CUDA device-side assert failures in the Hungarian matcher. \textbf{Resolution:} Re-index all JSON category IDs to $0$--$21$ before training.

\subsection{Device-Mismatched Positional Embeddings}
Cached positional embeddings in HybridEncoder can trigger a \texttt{cuda:0} vs. \texttt{cpu} mismatch. \textbf{Resolution:} Apply an explicit \texttt{.to(src\_flatten.device)} cast at inference time.

\subsection{Staged Checkpoint Lifecycle \& In-Place Autograd Guard}
In \texttt{det\_solver.py}, Stage 2 transitions require checking \texttt{(self.output\_dir / 'best\_stg1.pth').is\_file()} to prevent \texttt{FileNotFoundError}. In loss computations, in-place coordinate mutations must be replaced by tensor concatenation to avoid backward graph corruption, and divisors must clamp $N_{\text{boxes}} \ge 1$ against empty batches.

\section{Limitations \& Future Work}
Results reflect a single run per configuration due to compute limits. Future iterations will explore DINOv3 feature distillation, optical Sea-thru restoration, and physical ROS 2 integration on BlueROV2 platforms.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presented Blue Sentinel, an end-to-end underwater debris detection, edge optimization, and ecological decision-support platform powered by RT-DETRv4 with D-FINE. Fine-tuned on TrashCan-Instance for 58 epochs, the model reaches $\text{AP}@[0.50:0.95] = 0.517$, $\text{AP}@0.50 = 0.686$, and $\text{AP}@0.75 = 0.555$. Comparative benchmarking against YOLOv11 + MixStructureBlock + EMA highlights the strengths of transformer set prediction in high-IoU boundary fidelity. Coupled with TensorRT FP16 acceleration, an ROV simulator, persistence-gated entanglement alerts, and a cleanup decision optimizer, Blue Sentinel establishes a practical bridge between deep learning and operational ocean conservation.
